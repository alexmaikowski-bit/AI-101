Act II

Now — the build.

The seven moves.

Here's how the build actually happens.

STEP 01

Name the problem before you name the solution.

Define

Before you open Claude — write down, in plain language, what you're actually trying to do. "I want a microsite" isn't a problem; "my team keeps losing track of approved ideas across three docs" is.

  • Who is this for, and what moment do they encounter it? A pitch? A team Monday morning? A friend you're texting a link to?
  • What does success look like in one sentence? If you can't write that sentence, you're not ready to brief anything.
Rule of thumb

If your idea fits in a tweet, it's ready to build. If it takes a paragraph to explain the setup before the idea lands, keep editing.

STEP 02

Brain-dump in a voice note. Hand it to the model.

Dump

The fastest way from idea to brief is to talk, not type. Open the Claude app, hit the mic, and ramble for two to four minutes — audience, what exists, what inspires, what you're worried about. Don't edit yourself. Then ask the model to do three things:

  • Play back what you said in clean structure. Audience, problem, success state, tone, constraints.
  • Ask you the questions a good strategist would ask before drafting anything.
  • Only after you've answered, draft a prompt you can use to start the real build in a fresh chat.
→ TRY THIS OPENER

I'm going to ramble for a few minutes. When I'm done, play back what I said in structured form, then ask me the questions a senior strategist would ask before drafting anything. Don't make anything yet.

STEP 03

Show, don't tell. Feed it your taste.

References

References are where most AI builds win or lose their identity. The more particular your taste cues, the more the output will look like you and not a template — without them, models default to an aesthetic average (card-heavy, slightly purple, forgettable).

Screenshots Named aesthetic Anti-references Vibe word
  • Screenshots of sites you love. Three to six is plenty. Upload them directly.
  • Name the aesthetic out loud. "Editorial, like a Bloomberg long-read." "Brutalist with magazine energy." Specificity unlocks better instincts.
  • Anti-references. Tell it what to avoid by name. "Nothing that looks like a SaaS landing page from 2019."
  • Pick a vibe word and hold it. Confident. Quiet. Loud. Sharp. Refer back to it when something feels off.
A trick that works

Ask Claude to articulate the design direction back to you before it writes a single line of code. If those sentences don't make you nod, fix them now.

STEP 04

Bring the source material. All of it.

Sources

What separates a generic build from a great one isn't a clever brief — it's the material you bring alongside it. The richer the inputs, the sharper the output.

Decks Voice memos Real data Real copy Brand voice samples Web search
  • Decks, briefs, notes, voice memos. Even the rough version. Especially the rough version.
  • Real data and real copy. Placeholder data leads to placeholder thinking.
  • Brand voice samples. "Here are five captions that sound like us. Here are three that don't. Learn the difference."
  • Web search where the data is alive. Current stats, recent news, public sources. Bake citations in so the work is defensible.
The context principle

You are not bothering it. You are not "giving it too much." Front-load the work. The build will be ten times better for it.

STEP 05

Build the first cut. Then push back.

Iterate

Ask for an artifact — a single self-contained HTML page is the right starting point for most ideas. The first version will be a draft; your job now is editor, not briefer.

  • React in plain language. "This feels too AI-generated." "It's reading as a template." The model can take honest feedback.
  • Ask it to break its own pattern. "What's a more unique design language we could layer on this?"
  • Edit one thing at a time. Big "fix everything" requests produce muddled results. Tight, specific asks compound.
  • Save versions. If you wander somewhere worse, you can always come back.
→ USEFUL PHRASES

"Make this feel less like a template and more like something a human designed."

"Break the grid. I want intentional asymmetry, not symmetry."

"Cut everything that isn't load-bearing. Fewer ideas, executed harder."

STEP 06

Put it somewhere people can see it.

Host

Once you have an HTML file, getting it in front of someone is straightforward. A handful of paths — email, free hosting, internal team links — depending on who needs to see it and whether you'll iterate.

Email Claude public link Google Sites GitHub Pages Vercel
  • Email it. An HTML file as an attachment opens directly in any browser. Zero setup — fine for one-off sends.
  • Claude artifact link. Fastest path if you built it as an artifact. Click "Publish" and you get a public URL anyone can open without an account.
  • Google Sites. Host the HTML inside Google Workspace and share it with anyone in your org. Good for internal team distribution when you want a real URL but not the open internet.
  • GitHub Pages. Free. Drop your HTML into a repo, turn on Pages, you get a permanent URL like yourname.github.io/project.
  • Vercel. Drag-and-drop hosting with environment-variable support if you ever need it for API keys.
One thing to know

If your project calls an external AI service at runtime, don't paste an API key directly into a public HTML file — anyone can view source. For a quick share, you almost certainly don't need this. Start static.

STEP 07

Build an Open Companion.

Connect

The artifact you built is one moment. The Companion is the persistent version of it — an agent (or small agentic team) inside Open, trained on the same knowledge and ready for anyone on the team to pick up, push further, and feed with their own context.

  • Decide what knowledge feeds it. The same context you used to build the artifact — brief, deck, research, voice notes. Cleaner inputs make a sharper Companion.
  • One agent or a team? A single Companion is right for narrow use cases (one brief, one trend). An agentic team is better when the use case has distinct roles — strategist, planner, creative — that each need their own POV.
  • Set what it accepts as new input. Decide what people can drop in: only proprietary research, or public sources too. Clear rules make the Companion trustworthy.
  • Write its opening prompt. Frame the Companion the way you'd frame a strategy meeting — what's on the table, what we're solving for, how it can help.
The compounding move

The build is the moment. The Companion is the system — same knowledge, persistent, queryable, growing every time someone touches it.

The Interactive Brief

Replace the brief deck with a live link. Audience, insight, KPIs — clickable, scrollable, and the creative agency partner actually reads it because it's not 40 slides.

For / Briefing the creative agency
→ Drop into your coding agent 
I want to build an interactive brief microsite to replace the brief deck we'd normally send our creative agency partner.

Context:
- Brand: [BRAND NAME]
- Audience: [PRIMARY AUDIENCE]
- Strategic POV: [THE ONE-LINE INSIGHT THAT FRAMES THIS BRIEF]
- Business objective: [WHAT THE BRAND IS TRYING TO DO]
- KPIs: [HOW WE'LL MEASURE SUCCESS]

Build a single self-contained HTML page that flows through:
1. Hero with the strategic POV in big editorial Fraunces type
2. Who we're talking to — audience profile with behavior, mindset, what they're not (paragraph + short list)
3. What we're saying — message hierarchy: core idea, supporting beliefs, what we'd never say
4. Where we'll meet them — channel implications, moments, format cues the creative team needs to know
5. How we'll know it worked — KPIs with target ranges and why they matter

Design direction: editorial long-read, not SaaS landing page. Cream paper, ink type, neon cyan accents used sparingly. Fraunces serif for display, Inter Tight for body. No card patterns, no rainbow gradients.

Build a single self-contained HTML page that turns the strategy into a media-actionable map:

The Insight Microsite

One stat, turned into the editorial moment it actually deserves. Massive type, framed for the room — the kind of finding that survives the meeting and gets sent around after.

For / The finding that deserves better
→ Drop into your coding agent 
I want to build a single-page editorial microsite for one research finding. This is the kind of insight that usually dies on slide 47 of a deck — I want to give it the editorial moment it deserves.

Context:
- The finding: [PASTE THE INSIGHT / STAT — e.g., "64% of Gen Z women have used AI for skincare advice in the last 30 days, up from 18% a year ago"]
- Source: [WHERE THIS CAME FROM — research panel, MRI, internal study, etc.]
- Brand or category context: [WHO'S READING THIS / WHAT BRAND CONTEXT IT'S BEING READ THROUGH]

Build a single self-contained HTML page treating this finding the way a magazine treats a hero pull-quote:
1. Hero: the finding itself in massive editorial Fraunces type, treated for impact — line breaks for cadence, italic emphasis on the punch word
2. Context (one short paragraph): why this matters, what's driving it
3. Three implications for brand strategy — the "so what" for a senior planner
4. Our opinionated POV — one paragraph of confident point-of-view on what this means going forward. Take a stand.
5. Source and methodology in small mono type at the bottom

Design direction: editorial print, not stat-dashboard. Wallpaper magazine or The Drift energy. Cream paper, ink type, massive serif display, neon cyan accents only where they earn it. NO charts. NO icons. NO cards. Text and typography only.

The Whitespace Explorer

Map the category. See what every brand is already saying, who's getting served and who isn't, and where there's still room to plant a flag that costs less than fighting for the obvious.

For / Pitches that need a fresh angle
→ Drop into your coding agent 
I want to build a category whitespace map — a strategic doc that shows what's already claimed and where there's still room to plant a flag.

Context:
- Brand: [BRAND NAME]
- Category: [CATEGORY — e.g., "premium tequila", "home insurance", "energy drinks for women"]
- Main competitors: [LIST 3-5]
- The question I'm answering: [E.G., "where should we land for the relaunch?", "what's our reason to win against [COMPETITOR]?"]

Use web search to ground this in real positioning — pull actual recent campaigns, brand language, and ad creative where useful.

Build a single self-contained HTML page that includes:
1. Hero: the category landscape in one sentence — what's true about who owns what
2. The claimed territory — each main competitor's current positioning in a one-sentence verdict (what they're saying, what they own, what they're missing)
3. The audience map — who's getting served, who's underserved, where the cultural tension lives
4. The cultural moments and tensions the category is ignoring or fumbling
5. Three whitespace plays, each with: the territory we'd own, the audience pocket it speaks to, the cultural underpinning that makes it true now, a "what this would sound like" one-liner, and a defensibility test (could a competitor copy this easily?)
6. The honest verdict — which play is the best bet, and what would have to be true for it to work

Design direction: confident competitive map, not a McKinsey 2x2. Editorial layout, opinionated language. Fraunces serif for the plays, Inter Tight for analysis. Neon cyan accents on the whitespace itself.

Before you go

Three things to remember.

Hold these in your head — the rest follows.

01 / The collaborator

Not a peer. A master.

Closer to the best creative director and the best full-stack developer you've ever worked with — same person, infinite patience, zero ego, craft higher than most people you'd actually hire. Brief it that way: context, stakes, references, what good looks like, what to avoid. If a brief would confuse the best CD you know, it'll confuse this.

02 / The detail

Specificity beats cleverness.

"Make it pop" produces noise. "Increase the headline to 96px, use Fraunces italic on the verbs, reduce the body to a single column" produces a result. The model can't read your mind — it can only read what you actually wrote.

03 / The motive

Build things you'd use.

The best practice projects are the ones you'd actually open on a Tuesday morning. Solve a problem you have. The motivation compounds — the one that solves something real gets opened, iterated, sent around. The one that doesn't dies in a folder.
